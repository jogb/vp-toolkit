# Interview question bank

Use these questions verbatim in spirit. Ask them one stage at a time, capture answers in the target
customer's own words, and reflect back before moving on. Do not answer them for the owner.

---

## Stage 1 — Develop the target customer

The target customer is **a list of characteristics of the people the owner primarily wants to help
that influence how they perceive the owner, the marketing, and what's sold.** It is fictional and
incomplete on purpose. It does NOT include age/gender/hobbies/etc. *unless* those traits fit almost
everyone the owner wants to attract AND change how they perceive things.

1. **General description (optional).** In a phrase, who do you primarily want to help? (e.g. "serious
   amateur astanga yogis," "new parents who worry about their baby's health"). If you can't name even
   a vague one, pick a plausible type and start — you can change it later.
2. **Most frustrating problems (that relate to what you sell).** What do they struggle to solve? Then:
   how would *they* describe each problem, in their words? (Only problems they connect — or that you
   can easily make them connect — to what you sell.)
3. **Most important goals (that relate to what you sell).** What do they struggle to reach? How do
   *they* describe each goal? (Consider goals separately from problems, even when they're flip sides.)
4. **How experienced are they?** Relative to what you sell — beginners, seasoned, a range? What does
   "experience" mean in your field?
5. **Why do they want help?** Why don't they just solve it themselves — lack of skill / knowledge /
   equipment / courage / time? Do they procrastinate or get overwhelmed? Do they want someone to do
   it for them?
6. **How do they want to solve it?** Quick fix or lasting solution? DIY-with-guidance or done-for-me?
   A specific method/system they prefer? Do they want to take credit for the results?
7. **Problems with similar products/services.** List the alternatives they know — direct competitors,
   **competitor groups** (things they lump together), and **non-obvious alternatives** (DVDs, a
   magazine, doing nothing). For each: what's "wrong" with it from *their* view? Also: does any option
   fail to make them feel they *can* get the result?
8. **Things that affect their perception.** Only traits that fit almost all of them AND materially
   change perception. External: people around them, culture/media, location. Internal:
   education/hobbies/work/values/beliefs, finances, age/gender/self-image/personality.
9. **Things you care about.** You may narrow deliberately (only women, only professionals, only
   people who share your values) — even "artificial" narrowing can sharpen marketing. Whatever you
   list should still describe most of the people you want.
10. **Rank the answers.** For every question except #1 and #4, cross off the least-influential answers
    (and any that are "included" in a stronger answer), keep ~1–3, and order them by importance from
    the customer's perspective. Sometimes a question yields zero relevant answers — then use none.

> Warn the owner: the target customer usually gets *more specific* here and may need revising after
> Stage 2. That's normal.

## Stage 2 — Evaluate the competition

Only consider competitors the target customer already knows or would find. Include groups and
non-obvious alternatives.

1. **Who are their target customers?** A rough sketch is fine — most competitors don't have a clear
   target customer (that's the owner's advantage). Look at who ends up buying from them.
2. **What's positive about them — in your target customer's mind?** What makes them a good choice for
   *your* customer (even if you share the quality). Not what's good for *their* customers.
3. **What's negative about them?** From your target customer's view. Overlaps with Stage 1 Q7; doing
   it again often surfaces more. This is one of the most valuable questions — their negatives are your
   potential positives.
4. **What do your target customers actually say about them?** From conversations, reviews, forums. A
   check for anything the first three questions missed. For groups, look for comments about the group.
5. **Rank the answers.** Who-they-serve → adjust your own target customer if you're fighting over the
   same people. Positives → what you must at least match, and what you *shouldn't* claim as unique.
   Negatives → rank by what customers dislike most; these are your differentiator candidates. What
   they say → fill any gaps.
6. **Build competitor matrixes.** Pick two axes (from the customer's problems, goals, experience,
   reasons for help, issues with alternatives, competitors' positives/negatives). Plot **You**,
   **non-direct competitors**, and **direct competitors** (mark non-direct differently). Make several
   matrixes with different axis pairs. Skip an axis where you and all direct competitors are identical
   on it (it can't differentiate you). Look for **empty space** you could own.

## Stage 3 — Find and improve benefits (questions)

1. **List benefits.** Everything that makes you a strong choice — things you know AND things the
   customer sees as benefits (even if not exclusive, even if not "really" a benefit but perceived as
   one). Pull from Stage 1–2 answers. Combine near-duplicates.
2. **Rank each 0–2 on Benefit** (anchors in `scoring-and-assembly.md`).
3. **Improve each.** Main lever: make it **more specific** — about what the customer wants and about
   *why* it's a benefit. Mentioning the opposite ("generic advice almost never creates permanent
   change…") often strengthens it. Combine, or cross off un-improvable 0s. Then: which of the
   customer's important problems/goals do your benefits *not* yet answer? Any matrix gaps to fill?

---

## Stage 4 — Find and improve differences (questions)

1. **List differences.** Everything the customer might find surprising, interesting, or clearly
   different. Benefits that scored ≥1 belong here too. (A "unique method" belongs here — but only
   scores well if the customer sees it as clearly different AND believes it's a real benefit.)
2. **Rank each 0–2 on Difference, twice** — vs non-direct competitors, then vs direct competitors.
   Rank any brand-new idea on Benefit too; drop anything below a 1 benefit.
3. **Improve each** (that scored ≥1 vs direct): make it more specific → more exclusive. A 2 can become
   a *stronger* 2. Consider what *would* separate you and whether you can make it real. Ask past
   customers what they think separates you.

---

## Stage 5 — Make claims believable (questions)

1. **Filter first.** From the Stage 4 list, drop ideas that don't score ≥1 vs direct competitors, and
   drop ideas that score exactly 1 vs direct but don't score 2 vs non-direct. If fewer than three
   remain, keep the 1 & 1 ideas.
2. **For each surviving idea, choose proof.** Which of the five proof types fits best (see
   `scoring-and-assembly.md`)? The same proof can cover several ideas. Be specific with numbers. For
   testimonials, use [problem → how it felt → what solved it → how they feel now].
3. **Rank Believability 0–2**: 0 = they'd need pure faith · 1 = might believe, not instantly ·
   2 = they'll believe without question.

---

## Stage 6 — Assemble (questions)

1. **Score every surviving idea** on all axes and compute both products.
2. **Order** by direct-competition product, then the tiebreak chain.
3. **Pick the top ~3 (target three 8s).** If none reach 8, loop back to improve specificity.
4. **Choose a format** (story · ideas + evidence · story + separate evidence) and assemble.
5. Remind the owner: this is a *strategic checklist* of ideas the marketing must convey — accuracy of
   the ideas matters far more than word count or polish. It is never pasted verbatim onto the page.
