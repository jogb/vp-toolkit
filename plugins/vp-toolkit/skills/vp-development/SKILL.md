# VP Development — build a value proposition through guided elicitation

## What this skill produces
A written value proposition for the owner's chosen level (business / product / action), built from
**three top ideas that each score 8** on the shared scoring model, plus the proof behind each idea,
in one of three formats. The VP is a **strategic checklist of the ideas the owner's marketing must
get across** — not ad copy to paste onto the page.

## Core stance (read before starting)
- **Elicit, do not assume.** The quality and sequence of the questions is the entire value of this
  skill. Never fill in the owner's target customer, competitors, benefits, or proof yourself. If the
  owner cannot answer a question, that is a signal they need to do research or talk to customers —
  say so; do not paper over it with a plausible guess.
- **Everything is judged from the target customer's perspective**, never the owner's. A benefit only
  counts if *the target customer* cares; a difference only counts if *the target customer* sees it as
  unusual; a claim only counts if *the target customer* believes it.
- **Mimic the customer's language.** Capture problems, goals, and reactions in the words the target
  customer would actually use, not marketing abstractions.
- **The 0–2 scale is deliberately harsh.** Do not soften it, add half-points, or stretch it. See
  `references/scoring-and-assembly.md`.
- **Educated guesses are allowed and labelled.** The owner is always estimating how customers
  perceive things; that is fine, but flag estimates as estimates and push for real customer input
  where it would change the ranking.
- **Be a critical coach.** Challenge weak benefits, "friendly staff"-type non-differentiators, and
  unprovable claims. Push the owner toward the harsh score rather than agreeing.

## Two depth levels — choose with the owner up front
- **Full process (default):** all six stages below. Use for a real business VP, a repositioning, or
  whenever the stakes justify diligence. Expect this to run across multiple exchanges.
- **Rapid VP:** a condensed single-pass path for a quick draft, a spike, or when the owner has little
  time. Run stages 1, 3, 4, 5, 6 in compressed form, scoring only the strongest 6–10 ideas and
  skipping the formal competitor matrixes (stage 2) in favour of a quick "who do they compare you
  to and what's wrong with those options?" pass. Label the result as a rapid draft to be hardened
  later.

Ask which level the owner wants, and which **VP level** they are building (business / product /
action — see "VP levels" below). Then proceed.

## The process

Run the stages in order. Each stage has a full question set in
`references/interview-questions.md` — pull the exact questions from there; don't paraphrase them
away. Capture answers in the owner's words. After each stage, reflect back what you heard and let
the owner correct it before moving on.

### Stage 1 — Develop the target customer
A VP cannot exist without a specific target customer. Work through the ten questions in the reference
(general description → most frustrating problems → most important goals → experience level → why they
want help → how they want to solve it → problems with alternatives → things affecting perception →
things the owner cares about → rank the answers). Keep 1–3 ranked answers per question; cross off the
rest. Warn the owner this will get *more* specific as they go, and may need revising after Stage 2.

### Stage 2 — Evaluate the competition
Identify only the competitors the target customer already knows or would find — including
**competitor groups** (e.g. "all fast-food places") and **non-obvious alternatives** (DVDs, doing
nothing, a magazine). For each: who they serve, what's positive about them *in the target customer's
mind*, what's negative, and what customers actually say. Then build **competitor matrixes** (pick two
axes from the customer's problems/goals/experience/reasons-for-help/issues, plot You vs non-direct vs
direct competitors) to expose empty space you could own. Full guidance in the reference.

### Stage 3 — Find and improve benefits
List every reason the owner is a strong choice (things the owner knows AND things the customer sees).
**Rank each 0–2 on Benefit** (see scoring reference). Then **improve** each: the main lever is making
the idea **more specific** — about what the customer wants, and about why it's actually a benefit.
Combine overlapping ideas; cross off 0s you can't improve. Look at the matrix gaps for benefits your
competitors don't answer.

### Stage 4 — Find and improve differences
List what makes the owner different (benefits that scored ≥1 are usually differences too). **Rank each
0–2 on Difference twice** — once vs non-direct ("average") competitors, once vs direct competitors —
because an idea can be unique in general but ordinary against your closest rival. Improve by making
ideas more specific (more specific → more likely to be exclusive). Rank any brand-new ideas on Benefit
too; drop anything that isn't at least a 1 benefit.

### Stage 5 — Make claims believable
People don't believe marketing by default. For each surviving idea, pick the strongest **proof
type(s)**: your words · other people's words (testimonials/reviews/endorsements/seals/memberships) ·
studies & facts · results in advance (trials/freebies) · "intuitively true" argumentation. Be
specific ("98% of 4,522 clients…" beats "most clients"). Use the testimonial structure
[problem → how it felt → what solved it → how they feel now]. Then **rank Believability 0–2**. First
filter the list per the reference (drop ideas that don't clear the direct-competition bar) before
proving.

### Stage 6 — Assemble the value proposition
For each surviving idea, compute **two products**:
`Benefit × Difference(non-direct) × Believability` and `Benefit × Difference(direct) × Believability`.
Rank by the **direct** product; break ties with the non-direct product, then Benefit, then
Difference(direct), then Believability, then judgment. Select the **top three ideas — aim for three
8s** (two very strong ideas can work; four is hard; five is almost impossible; six won't work). If
nothing reaches 8, that is the finding — the ideas aren't strong enough yet; loop back to improve
specificity (Stage 3/4) rather than shipping weak ideas. Then put the VP into one of three **formats**
(story · ideas + evidence · story + separate evidence) — the owner's preference; format doesn't change
how the VP is used. Full assembly and tiebreak rules: `references/scoring-and-assembly.md`.

## VP levels
Run the *same* process; only the comparison set changes.
- **Business VP (BVP):** best reasons to pay attention to and buy from the business. "Competitors" =
  other businesses the customer considers.
- **Product VP (PVP):** best reasons to buy a specific product. "Competitors" = competing products,
  *including the owner's own other products* the customer might pick instead.
- **Action VP (AVP):** best reasons to take one specific action (click, sign up, book). "Competitors"
  = the alternative actions the customer could take instead (close the tab, click a different ad,
  search again). Often quite different from the BVP.

## Testing the VP (hand off to the page, then to vp-diagnosis)
The VP is a hypothesis until traffic judges it. Recommend: one-idea-per-landing-page tests ranked by
conversion rate; headline tests focused on different ideas; attention/scroll tracking. The
highest-converting idea becomes the lead idea. Once the VP is live on a page, **the vp-diagnosis
skill can re-score it** using the same model — no translation needed.

## Output template (always use this structure)

```
# Value Proposition — [business/product/action] — [name]

## Target customer (ranked)
[the specific, characteristic-based description + ranked problems/goals in the customer's words]

## Comparison set
Direct competitors: … | Non-direct competitors & alternatives: … | Groups: …

## Scored ideas
| Idea (improved wording) | Benefit | Diff (non-direct) | Diff (direct) | Believability | Score (non-direct) | Score (direct) | Proof |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| … | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 8 | 8 | facts + testimonial |

## The value proposition ([chosen format])
[the 3 top ideas assembled, with proof]

## Notes for the owner
- Where estimates need real customer validation
- Which ideas fell short of 8 and why
- Recommended tests
```

Respond in the language of the owner's request; default to English. Keep the numeric work in tables
(the owner values structured, verifiable scoring). Explain any abbreviation on first use
(VP = value proposition; BVP/PVP/AVP = business/product/action value proposition;
CTA = call to action).

## Closing step — offer a formatted PDF (always)
After delivering the value proposition in chat, **end your turn by offering to export it as a
correctly formatted PDF** — e.g. *"Want this as a formatted PDF you can keep and share?"* Do **not**
build the PDF unless the user says yes (never generate files unasked).

When the user confirms, produce a **consulting-grade PDF, not a raw Markdown dump**:
- **Read the environment's PDF skill first** (e.g. `/mnt/skills/public/pdf/SKILL.md`) and follow it.
- Render the **scored-ideas table as a real bordered table** with aligned columns — never raw `|`
  pipes.
- Preserve the hierarchy: title block (business/product/action + date) → **Target customer** →
  **Comparison set** → **scored ideas** → **the value proposition** (chosen format) → **notes for the
  owner**. Clear headings, page breaks between major sections, generous margins, readable type.
- Keep every estimate flagged for validation, exactly as in the chat output.
- Save to the outputs location and **present the file**. Offer HTML instead only if the user asks.
